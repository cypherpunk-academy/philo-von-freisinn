There was a cook everyone called the Miracle Cook. Not because he performed any magic. Because people went quiet after his meals and couldn't quite say why.
One day an invitation came. A shipowner wanted to hold a banquet aboard his ship, a buffet for two hundred guests. The commission was simple: as good as you can make it. One year to prepare.
The Miracle Cook accepted. He made one condition. He wanted a small dossier on every guest. Where they came from. What work they did. How their life had gone. What had happened to them, and what they had made of it.
The host laughed, took it for an eccentricity — and had the files drawn up.
Nine months over the files
For nine months the Miracle Cook sat over those papers. He read, and when something was missing he sent his assistant out, who brought back whatever could be found. Sometimes it was a small thing: that one of them had grown up on a farm. That one had lost her mother early. That one had never felt full, though there had always been enough.
In the end he had a picture. Not of two hundred names. Of two hundred people.
Only then did he begin to plan. And he didn't do it with his head. He did it from feeling, out of the many years in which he had cooked and, in cooking, come to know his guests.
With some of them he was guessing. He knew that. And he took pleasure in not getting things too exact.
The picture of the buffet
What finally stood before him was not a menu but a picture: an enormous buffet in courses that would transform itself over the course of the evening. Some things would vanish, others would grow back in their place. It was meant to breathe.
Now he assembled his cooks — the ones who could do exactly what he had imagined. With them he went through both: the people and the dishes. Who was coming. What should appear, and when. In what order.
Three days before, they began buying. At markets, at farms, from people they knew. There was haggling and cursing and making up again.
Everything they laid in their crates had once lived or stood somewhere. The fish had swum. The grain had stood in a field for a year and taken the rain and the drought. The lamb had had a name that nobody at the market remembered any more.
Then the ship came into harbour. The kitchen team went aboard and had twenty-four hours. Two more joined them who were responsible for nothing but the look of it: that the buffet be sensibly approachable, that it could change, that it be adorned without making itself important.
The speech
That evening the host spoke first. Then the cook. Not for long. Just enough that most of them wanted to try it all.
He said nothing about having studied them. Nothing about the nine months he had spent considering what should go on that table, and for whom.
Then the music started, and the buffet was open.
The scramble
And then they threw themselves at it.
It became a scramble. People pressed forward, people cleared plates. Something went on the floor. One man had far too much on his plate and left half of it. One guest was sick. Some did not get enough of the thing they wanted, because someone else had emptied it — someone it meant nothing to, who took it only because it happened to be standing there.
For the buffet was not excessively full. It was calculated exactly for the people present. Enough for everyone — but only if each of them had taken what was there for him.
Everything happened that you could imagine happening. And it did happen.
Afterwards
Afterwards it looked like the aftermath of a battle. People cleared up.
What went into the bins that evening — scraped off plates, picked up from the floor, opened and left standing — would have fed the ship's crew for three days.
The second cook came over, looked at him sadly and said: "We put so much work into it. Into every single thing. And they just devoured it. Everyone took as much as he wanted."
The Miracle Cook nodded. He looked out into the empty hall and said:
"I'm quite content with how it turned out. I didn't want to take this commission at all, in fact. But then something came to me.
I wanted to know how it is for the gods.
They give us our abilities. And precisely the abilities each of us needs in order to meet the needs of the others. A whole network of abilities and needs, one fitted into the next. There would be enough for everyone. It is measured out exactly.
And then egotism comes in between. That is man, and I feel splendid about it."
He fell silent. The second cook looked at him.
"I think it's the same for them. That's what I wanted to find out for once."
That night, far out, the waste went over the side. The sea took it back and said nothing.
